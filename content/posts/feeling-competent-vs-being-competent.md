I recall a time several years ago where something went wrong in a system. Users reported the issue. I was able to quickly narrow down the issue by looking at logs. Not just looking at logs but knowing where to look and more importantly knowing what to look for. It felt great being able to narrow down the issue and ultimately resolving it. 

# Don’t be afraid to be wrong — until you’re not

I’ve learned that being wrong about something shouldn’t stop you from trying again — at least not until you’ve learned why you were wrong.

I’ve found myself in situations where I backed the wrong choice. Others backed the better one at the time. In software engineering, this often comes down to technical decisions: do you choose approach A or approach B? And if you choose the wrong one, what does that say about you?

Does backing a less favourable approach make you less competent?

I’ve been in a situation where I proposed a solution for a new project — one I had successfully used before. I assumed it would apply just as well here. It didn’t. The solution wasn’t received positively, and it was ultimately rejected.

The details of the problem aren’t important. What mattered was the reaction. Rejection stings. It hits the ego. Especially when the intent was honest and the experience was real.

But being wrong in that moment didn’t make me an incompetent engineer. It exposed a gap — not in ability, but in context. Experience teaches you patterns, but competence also requires knowing when those patterns don’t apply.

Not everything you build will be received well.
And rejection doesn’t infer incompetence — how you respond to it reveals far more.

# Don't hang onto your ideas

When a solution is rejected, the best thing you can often do is let it go entirely.

And by that, I mean stripping out the ego and moving on.

Ideas and opinions are temporary.
The way you engage with your team lasts much longer.

I’ll be the first to admit that I haven’t always been good at this. When you believe in a solution, rejection can make you defensive. You get frustrated. You replay the conversation. You try to justify why you were right.

But over time, I’ve learned to pause and ask myself a few simple questions.

Will this matter in three months?
Is holding onto this worth my wellbeing?
Is team cohesion more important than being right?

Letting go of an idea — or having it rejected — is not a sign of incompetence. More often, it’s a sign that the work is bigger than any one person’s contribution.

Rejection shouldn’t prevent you from contributing again.
And acceptance of ideas is not a reliable measure of competence.

That’s something I’ve had to work hard at — learning to let ideas go without tying my sense of worth to their outcome.
